01 — THE BASICS

What Are Vaccines?

Understanding the science behind one of the most important applications of biotechnology.

💉
Science that protects lives

A Lesson for Your Immune System

Vaccines are biological preparations that help the immune system recognize and fight specific pathogens, such as viruses and bacteria.

They contain antigens or genetic instructions that allow the body to produce a safe immune response. This helps the immune system recognize the pathogen if it encounters it in the future.

Vaccines can prevent certain infectious diseases and reduce the risk of severe illness.

Did you know?
Vaccination prepares your immune system to recognize a specific threat without requiring you to experience the full disease.
02 — THE SCIENCE

How Do Vaccines Work?

Vaccines train your immune system through a series of biological responses.

01
💉

Vaccine Enters

The vaccine introduces an antigen or genetic instructions that help the body recognize a specific pathogen.

02
🧫

Immune Recognition

The immune system recognizes the antigen as foreign and activates an immune response.

03
🛡️

Antibodies Develop

B cells can produce antibodies that help recognize and fight the specific pathogen.

04
🧠

Immune Memory

Memory B cells and T cells help the body respond faster during future exposure.

03 — VACCINE TECHNOLOGY

Types of Vaccines

Different biotechnology methods are used to develop vaccines. Search or click each card to learn more.

🦠

Live-Attenuated

Uses a weakened form of the living pathogen to stimulate an immune response.

The weakened pathogen can still replicate but is modified so it does not usually cause disease in healthy people. Examples include MMR and varicella vaccines. These vaccines may not be suitable for some people with severely weakened immune systems.
🧪

Inactivated

Uses pathogens that have been killed or inactivated so they cannot reproduce.

The pathogen cannot replicate or cause infection. Some inactivated vaccines require multiple doses or booster shots to maintain protection. Examples include inactivated polio and hepatitis A vaccines.
🔬

Subunit / Recombinant

Uses only specific parts of a pathogen, such as proteins or antigens.

Recombinant DNA technology can produce pathogen proteins in other cells. These proteins are purified and used as vaccine antigens. Examples include hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
🧬

mRNA Vaccines

Uses messenger RNA to provide instructions for cells to produce an antigen.

The mRNA is delivered into cells, often using lipid nanoparticles. Cells temporarily produce the antigen, which triggers an immune response. The mRNA is broken down afterward and does not change a person's DNA. Example: Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine.
🧫

Viral Vector

Uses a modified harmless virus to deliver genetic instructions for an antigen.

The modified vector carries genetic instructions into cells. The cells then produce an antigen that triggers an immune response. Example: Oxford-AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine.
🧬

DNA Vaccines

Uses a DNA plasmid containing instructions for producing a specific antigen.

The DNA plasmid enters cells, where its genetic instructions can be used to produce an antigen. This antigen is then recognized by the immune system.

04 — FROM LAB TO COMMUNITY

How Are Vaccines Developed?

Vaccine development involves scientific research, laboratory testing, clinical trials, and continuous safety monitoring.

🔍

1. Research and Discovery

Scientists study the pathogen and identify antigens that can trigger an immune response.

🧪

2. Preclinical Testing

Laboratory and appropriate animal studies evaluate the vaccine's potential safety and immune response.

👥

3. Clinical Trials

Phase I evaluates initial safety, Phase II examines dosage and immune response, and Phase III evaluates safety and effectiveness in larger groups.

📋

4. Regulatory Approval

Health authorities review scientific evidence before deciding whether a vaccine can be authorized.

🏥

5. Distribution and Monitoring

Approved vaccines are distributed and monitored for safety and effectiveness even after public use.

05 — BIOTECHNOLOGY IN ACTION

Why Is Biotechnology Important?

Modern biotechnology improves how scientists design, produce, and study vaccines.

🧬

Genetic Engineering

Scientists can modify genetic material and use recombinant DNA technology to produce specific vaccine antigens.

🔬

Advanced Laboratory Techniques

Biotechnology helps researchers identify pathogens, study immune responses, and evaluate vaccine performance.

⚗️

Improved Production

Cell culture, fermentation, and other biological processes help produce vaccine components on a larger scale.

🌍

Public Health Protection

Vaccine technology helps prevent infectious diseases, reduce severe illness, and support disease control programs worldwide.

06 — CRITICAL EVALUATION

Benefits, Risks & Ethical Issues

Vaccines offer major public health benefits, but their development and distribution also involve important challenges.

✓ Benefits

  • Prevent many infectious diseases.
  • Reduce the risk of severe illness and death.
  • Help protect communities through population immunity.
  • Support disease control and, in some cases, elimination.
  • Advances in biotechnology allow new vaccine designs.

! Challenges & Risks

  • Some vaccines cause temporary side effects, such as fever or soreness.
  • Rare but serious adverse reactions can occur.
  • Cold-chain requirements can make distribution difficult.
  • Vaccine production and waste can affect the environment.
  • Research, testing, and manufacturing can be expensive.

♡ Ethical Issues

  • People should receive accurate information before vaccination.
  • Informed consent and personal autonomy must be respected.
  • Vaccines should be accessible regardless of income.
  • Clinical trials must protect participants' safety and rights.
  • Public health policies must balance individual rights and community protection.

PHILIPPINE CASE STUDY

Vaccination in the Philippines

The Department of Health (DOH) implements the National Immunization Program to protect Filipinos against vaccine-preventable diseases.

Childhood immunization includes vaccines against diseases such as measles, polio, tuberculosis, and hepatitis B.

However, challenges such as vaccine hesitancy, misinformation, limited healthcare access, and distribution difficulties can affect vaccination coverage.

This shows why scientific innovation must be supported by public education, accessible healthcare, and responsible government policies.
